# Decision memo: Cincinnati 311 service performance

**To:** Director, Department of Public Services (primary); Director, Department of Buildings and Inspections (secondary)
**Analysis window:** requests created 2023-01-01 to 2026-08-31
**Data as of:** 2026-09-18
**Source:** City of Cincinnati 311 Non-Emergency Service Requests, 517,287 records

---

## Recommendation

**Fund additional bulky-collection capacity for June through September, and do not lengthen the 14-day commitment.**

Bulky collection misses its commitment in a concentrated, predictable window every year. In 2024, 84 percent of the service's annual missed commitments fell in four months. In 2026 so far, 79 percent of misses have fallen in three. The service performs at 95 to 99 percent for the rest of the year.

This is a seasonal capacity problem with a known shape, not a service in general difficulty.

---

## The decision this answers

Where should the city add capacity, and where should it instead reset a service commitment it is not meeting?

Two candidates emerged. They need opposite responses.

| | Bulky collection (`MTL-FRN`) | Residential building inspection (`BLD-RES`) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Volume | 117,670 requests, 22.7% of all 311 intake | 16,782 requests |
| Commitment | 14 days | 365 days |
| Failure pattern | Sharp, seasonal, recovers every autumn | Persistent, accumulating, does not recover |
| Overdue open cases | 1,527, median 58 days past due | 3,389, median 420 days past due |
| Recommended response | **Add capacity** | **Triage the backlog, then revisit the commitment** |

---

## Evidence

### 1. The failure is seasonal and it repeats

Bulky collection on-time rate by month:

| | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug | Sep | Oct | Nov | Dec |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2024 | 96.1 | 97.3 | 97.9 | 97.4 | 97.6 | 89.2 | 62.8 | 36.6 | 71.3 | 93.0 | 94.3 | 95.9 |
| 2025 | 86.6 | 65.4 | 63.1 | 64.3 | 57.1 | 14.9 | 8.5 | 5.1 | 69.9 | 99.8 | 91.8 | 94.6 |
| 2026 | 95.6 | 99.7 | 97.3 | 95.7 | 91.8 | 89.8 | 76.2 | 68.8 | | | | |

2025 was an extreme year: a seven-month decline beginning in February, bottoming at 5.1 percent in August, with a median close time of 24 days against a 14-day commitment. It recovered in October.

2026 is materially better. August 2026 closed at a 12-day median, the fastest August of the three years. The service has improved. It still degrades on the same seasonal curve.

### 2. Volume alone does not explain it

Comparing March with July in each year, holding the service constant:

| Year | March | July | Volume change | On-time change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2024 | 2,699 at 97.9% | 3,293 at 62.8% | +22% | -35.1 points |
| 2025 | 3,176 at 63.1% | 3,249 at 8.5% | +2% | -54.6 points |
| 2026 | 3,575 at 97.3% | 3,917 at 76.2% | +10% | -21.1 points |

July 2026 carried 10 percent more volume than March and lost 21 points. In 2025 the volume difference was 2 percent and the loss was 55 points.

**The number of requests arriving is not sufficient to explain the drop.** Something about summer operations is. Candidates the city can test internally and this dataset cannot: crew availability during leave season, competing seasonal workload such as yard waste and mowing, heavier or bulkier summer item mix, or vehicle and equipment availability.

This is the single most important open question in the analysis, and it determines how the capacity should be added rather than whether.

### 3. Demand seasonality is intensifying

| Year | Jan–Feb average intake | Jun–Aug average intake | Summer lift |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2024 | 2,232 | 2,874 | +29% |
| 2025 | 2,066 | 3,060 | +48% |
| 2026 | 2,188 | 3,532 | **+61%** |

The peak is growing relative to the trough. A capacity plan built on the 2024 seasonal profile will be undersized by 2027.

### 4. Lengthening the commitment has already been tried, and proved nothing

A 30-day commitment replaced the 14-day one for this service from approximately September 2025 through March 2026, then reverted.

It was in force only during the months when the service is naturally fast. Median close times inside that window were 12, 6, 5, 5 and 4 days, all of which would have met the original 14-day commitment.

The 30-day requests show a 96 percent on-time rate against 42 percent for 14-day requests in the same year. **That comparison is confounded and should not be used.** The tier and the season are entangled: the longer commitment applied only to the easy months. It provides no evidence that a longer commitment would help in summer, because it was never tested in summer.

## Recommendations

### R1. Add seasonal bulky-collection capacity, June through September

**Target:** restore July and August on-time performance to the spring baseline of the same year.

In 2026, spring (March to May) ran at 94.9 percent and summer (July to August) at 72.5 percent, a gap of 22.4 points. Closing it would have delivered approximately **1,568 additional requests on time** across those two months.

**Sizing requires city data this analysis does not have:** crew hours per route, tonnage per run, vehicle availability, and current summer overtime. Those inputs belong to Public Services. This analysis establishes the size and timing of the gap, not the cost of closing it.

**Before committing recurring spend,** run the diagnostic in evidence point 2. If the constraint is competing seasonal workload rather than absolute crew hours, the answer may be schedule sequencing rather than additional staff, at materially lower cost.

### R2. Do not lengthen the 14-day commitment

The 2025 to 2026 trial provides no evidence for it, for the reason given above. Lengthening the promise would raise the reported on-time rate without changing when a resident's furniture is collected.

If the commitment is revisited later, test it during June to September, where the constraint actually binds.

### R3. Triage the residential building inspection backlog

3,389 residential inspection requests are open and past their committed date. 1,929 of them, 57 percent, are more than a year overdue, and the median overdue case has been past due for 420 days.

A commitment exceeded by 420 days at the median is not operative. Before any capacity discussion, the department needs a case review establishing which of these represent live work and which are records that were never closed out. The distinction cannot be made from this dataset.

Three other building codes show the same signature: fire escape inspections with 388 overdue and **zero** within a year, commercial code enforcement at a 612-day median, and development permits at 661 days. These look like abandoned records rather than a queue.

The Building Department holds **51.5 percent of all overdue requests citywide on 12.1 percent of intake.** No citywide improvement is possible without addressing it.

### R4. Take no action on two things that look like problems and are not

**Neighbourhood variation in pothole repair.** On-time rates run 85.7 to 97.6 percent across 42 neighbourhoods, with a median close time of 2 to 3 days everywhere. The spread is detectable at these volumes and operationally trivial. There is no service-equity issue in pothole repair, and a targeted intervention would spend effort on a difference that does not affect residents.

**Web self-service as a source of duplicate requests.** Internet intake is 65.2 percent of volume with a 13.7 percent duplicate rate. The 311 call centre is 22.2 percent of volume with **14.4 percent**, slightly higher. The channel is not the cause. Duplicates concentrate in property-condition services, from 15.0 percent for litter to 19.7 percent for tall grass and weeds, which is consistent with several residents reporting the same visible problem. If duplicates are worth reducing, the intervention is showing existing reports at the point of submission, across all channels.

## Measuring whether R1 worked

**Primary measure:** bulky-collection on-time rate for July and August, against the March to May baseline of the same year.

**Why that comparison and not year on year:** the service improved substantially between 2025 and 2026 for reasons unrelated to any intervention. Comparing 2027 summer against 2026 summer would credit an intervention with whatever that underlying trend continues to deliver. Using the same year's spring as the reference holds crew, fleet and process broadly constant and isolates the seasonal gap, which is what the intervention targets.

**Supporting measures:** median days to close in July and August; count of missed commitments in June to September; overdue open requests at 30 September.

**What this design can and cannot establish.** A before-and-after comparison cannot establish causation. Weather, staffing changes, and demand shifts all move these numbers independently. If the city wants evidence of effect rather than association, the intervention should be staggered across collection districts so that districts receiving it in the first year can be compared against those receiving it in the second. That is a design decision to make before implementation, not after.

**Definitions are already fixed.** `reports/metric_dictionary.md` specifies the on-time rate, its population and its exclusions, so a follow-up measurement will compute the same thing this analysis did.

## Limitations

1. **The mechanism behind the summer failure is not identified.** Volume is insufficient. The likely causes require operational data the city holds and this dataset does not contain.

2. **Costs are not estimated.** No crew, vehicle or overtime figures appear in this analysis, and none have been assumed. Any cost-benefit case must be built on inputs supplied by Public Services.

3. **The publisher's field definition conflicts with the data.** The dictionary states the completion clock starts at first response; the data starts it at submission on 517,214 of 517,214 testable rows. The observed behaviour is used throughout. See `reports/source_definitions.md`.

4. **Closure codes are undefined.** The city publishes no value list for request status. Codes such as `CLOS-NO` and `ABAT-OWN` are treated as completed service. Under the alternative reading that they represent no service delivered, the citywide on-time rate falls from 72.5 percent to 61.2 percent. The headline figure should be read as a range.

5. **Trend reporting covers 13.3 percent of volume.** Only four services held a stable commitment across the window. In the rest the commitment changed mid-period, and a trend would mix a moving promise into a performance measure.

6. **2026 is a partial year**, January to August, and long-commitment services have no mature 2026 cohort at all. Residential building inspection cannot be evaluated for 2026 because no request created in 2026 is yet due.

7. **311 records reports, not incidents.** Request volume reflects what residents choose to report and through which channel, not the underlying rate of problems in the city.

8. **A contextual explanation for 2025 was considered and excluded.** A publicly reported figure attributing a reduction in illegal dumping to a local programme was self-reported by the programme operator with no stated methodology, and the timing did not fit: the decline began before the programme's season opened and the recovery came while it was still running. It is not used.

9. **One city, one window, no causal claims.** Nothing here generalises to other municipalities.

## Reproducibility

Every figure traces to SQL in `reports/analysis_tables__2026-09-18.md`, computed against a star schema built and tested in `src/model.py`, from an immutable snapshot recorded with its filter, pull timestamp, row count and SHA-256 hash.

The population reconciles exactly: all 517,287 records fall into one of seven mutually exclusive outcome buckets, and the model reproduces the pre-model on-time rate to two decimal places.
